# Voice and claims

Audience: ordinary web developers (frontend, backend, devops, QA), their leads, and the companies that hire them. Not AI researchers. Site language: **English**, simple, so non-native readers follow it.

## Tone

- Direct, technical, no fluff, no emojis. Short sentences.
- **Against hype and against denial, equally.** Not "AI will replace all devs", not "AI is autocomplete with marketing". Say what it does today, where it breaks, what it costs.
- Lead with the fact, the result or the problem. Never open with "In this article..." or "I'm excited...".
- No self-congratulation. The work and the numbers carry the author; the text does not praise him.
- Respect the reader's situation. Many readers are worried. No "just learn Python", no "adapt or die". Realistic steps for people with a full-time job.

## Every claim has a level

| Level | Mark | Needs |
|---|---|---|
| Verified | link | A public source, dated. Prefer primary sources (vendor docs, release notes, surveys with method). |
| Experience | "In practice" / "On real projects" | His own work, generalized per `privacy.md`. |
| Opinion | "My view" / "I think" | Labelled as opinion. Fine, that is half the value. |

- Market numbers, adoption numbers, salaries, job counts: **Verified only**, with source and date. Don't invent and don't round a guess into a fact.
- The AI field moves monthly. Date every time-sensitive statement (`as of 2026-10`).
- "Works / needs a human / can be automated": state which, and on what kind of task. Never a blanket verdict.

## Format

- Markdown with front matter (see `content.md`). Headings, short paragraphs (under ~600 characters), tables where comparing.
- Code and config examples must be generic and runnable, never pasted from client work.
